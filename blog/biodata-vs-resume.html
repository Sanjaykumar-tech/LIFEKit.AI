Biodata vs Resume: What's the Difference?

They sound similar. They're used for completely different things. Using the wrong one can cost you a job — or a match.

📖 4 min read ✍️ LIFEKit Team 📅 January 2025

Every week someone asks: "Should I send a biodata or a resume?" The two are confused constantly — especially in India, where both terms are used for different documents.

Here's the shortest possible answer:

Biodata is for marriage. Resume is for jobs. They are not interchangeable.

Now the longer answer — because the details matter.

The core difference

A biodata tells a family who you are as a person. It covers personal details — age, height, religion, family background, education, occupation, contact.

A resume tells an employer what you can do. It covers skills, work history, achievements, education, and impact. Nothing personal beyond contact info.

Side-by-side comparison

Aspect Biodata Resume
Purpose Marriage / matrimonial match Job application
Audience Prospective family Recruiter / hiring manager
Photo Usually included Not in India (Western countries: sometimes)
Personal details Age, height, religion, caste, marital status Not included
Family section Essential — parents, siblings Never
Focus Who you are What you've done
Length 1 page 1 page (fresher) to 2 pages (experienced)
Tone Personal, warm, traditional Professional, formal, achievement-focused

When to use a biodata

You send a biodata when:

  • Arranging a marriage through family / relatives
  • Sharing your profile on a matrimonial site
  • Responding to a "send biodata" request from a prospective family
  • Sending to a matchmaker, priest, or community elder coordinating matches

In these contexts, a resume would feel cold and out of place. Families want to know about your background, not your project experience.

When to use a resume

You send a resume when:

  • Applying for a job (any industry)
  • Reaching out to a recruiter on LinkedIn
  • Applying to internships, fellowships, or grad programs
  • Pitching yourself to a client (freelancers)

A biodata here would be bizarre. Recruiters don't care about your father's occupation or your height.

What goes in a biodata

  • Full name
  • Photo
  • Date of birth + age
  • Height
  • Religion / caste / community (if relevant to your family)
  • Mother tongue
  • Education — degree, college, year
  • Occupation + annual income
  • Father's name + occupation
  • Mother's name + occupation
  • Siblings — number, marital status
  • Contact — phone, email, address
  • Hobbies (brief, specific)

What goes in a resume

  • Full name + job title
  • Email, phone, location, LinkedIn
  • Professional summary (2–3 lines)
  • Work experience — company, role, dates, achievements
  • Education — degree, college, year, GPA (optional)
  • Skills — technical and soft
  • Projects (especially for freshers)
  • Certifications (optional)
  • Awards (optional)

The overlap — and where people mess up

A few things appear on both:

  • Name
  • Education
  • Occupation
  • Contact

That's it. Everything else is different.

People mess up by doing the opposite: they send a resume to a prospective family (too cold) or a biodata to a recruiter (too personal).

Can you use one for the other?

Technically yes. Practically, no.

A biodata sent to a recruiter would immediately signal "this person doesn't understand professional norms." A resume sent to a marriage prospect would feel distant and off-putting.

Two different documents. Two different audiences. Two different purposes.

The 10-second rule

If you're confused about which to use, ask: Who's reading this?

  • Hiring manager → resume
  • Family / matchmaker → biodata

That's the entire decision.

One last thing

In India, the word "biodata" is sometimes used loosely to mean "resume" — usually by older generations or in smaller towns. If a recruiter asks for a "biodata," they probably mean a resume. Ask to clarify.

When in doubt, match the format the other person expects. That's what gets you the job (or the match).
