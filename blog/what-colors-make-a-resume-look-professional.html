What Colors Make a Resume Look Professional?

The science of resume colors — plus 3 combinations that always work and 5 that will get you rejected.

📖 5 min read ✍️ LIFEKit Team 📅 January 2025

You've written a great resume. The content is solid. The format is clean. But you look at it and think… it feels flat.

So you add a splash of color. Maybe blue headings. A red accent. A green sidebar.

And now it looks worse than before.

Here's the truth: resume color is a narrow window. Use the wrong shade and you look unprofessional. Use too much and you look amateur. But use the right ones — subtly — and you look polished and modern.

The 90/10 rule

The rule

90% of your resume should be black text on white. Only 10% should have color — headings, a thin accent bar, or a subtle underline.

Anything more than 10% color = amateur. Anything less than 5% = flat. Hit the sweet spot and it feels deliberate.

The 3 combinations that always work

Always works

Classic Navy

Black text + navy blue headings. Timeless. Reads confident. Works for any industry — finance, law, tech, consulting.

Always works

Monochrome

Black text + dark grey headings + light grey dividers. Zero risk. Modern minimal. Perfect for tech and design roles.

Always works

Deep Forest

Black + dark green accents. Stands out without shouting. Great for sustainability, healthcare, education, NGOs.

Any of the three above will make your resume look professional. Pick the one that matches your industry.

The 5 colors you should never use

Avoid

Bright Red

Red signals danger, urgency, and stress. It reads as aggressive in a professional context. Small red accents can work — but large red areas look alarming.

Avoid

Neon Orange

Eye-catching, yes. But it reads as a highlighter, not a resume. Use only if you're applying to a startup with an intentionally loud brand.

Avoid

Yellow

Almost invisible on white paper. Terrible contrast. Even for headings, yellow doesn't stand out — it disappears.

Risky

Bright Pink

Bold and memorable — but polarizing. Works if you're in fashion, beauty, or a pink-branded company. Otherwise it distracts.

Risky

Bright Green

Reads as "unfinished" or "draft." Darker forest green is safer. Bright green feels like a design template gone wrong.

How to use color in a resume — 3 exact rules

Rule 1 — Color only the headings

Your name, contact info, and headings (like "Experience", "Education") can have color. Body text? Always black. Never color body paragraphs.

Rule 2 — One color, not three

Pick one accent color. Use it everywhere consistently. Multi-color resumes look like a middle-school project.

Rule 3 — Make sure it prints in black and white

Some recruiters print resumes. Test yours in grayscale. If your headings look like vague grey blobs, your color is too light. Navy and forest green pass this test. Bright orange doesn't.

Quick test

Print your resume on a black-and-white printer. If the person holding it can still tell what's a heading and what's body text — your color choice is good.

What about background colors?

Don't. A light grey or cream background can look elegant on a screen, but it:

  • Wastes printer ink for the recruiter
  • Often gets stripped by ATS software
  • Looks dated

White background. Always.

What about a colored sidebar?

Two-column resumes with a colored sidebar are popular on design platforms (Canva, etc.). They look great on screen — but many ATS systems can't read them properly.

If you're applying through an online portal, use a single-column format. If you're sending directly to a human (LinkedIn message, referral), a subtle colored sidebar can be fine.

When in doubt, choose boring. A boring resume that reaches a human beats a beautiful resume that never does.

The final formula

Here's the entire system, in one line:

White background + black body text + one dark accent color on headings = professional resume.

That's it. Navy, forest green, or dark grey. Pick one. Use it consistently. Done.

One last thing

Recruiters spend 6–8 seconds on your resume on first glance. That's it. In those 6 seconds, color either helps them scan faster — or distracts them from your experience.

Use color to guide the eye, not to decorate. That's the whole art of it.
